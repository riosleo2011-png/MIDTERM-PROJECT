Harlem Plaza Redesign

Reimagining Montefiore Square as a safer, greener, and more welcoming public space for residents, visitors, and neighborhood activity.

Explore the Project

About the Project

This project reimagines Montefiore Square as a safer, greener, and more welcoming public space for everyday community life. Located in Harlem at the intersection of Broadway, West 138th Street, and Hamilton Place, the plaza is highly visible and heavily used — yet most people move through it rather than stay in it.

The redesign explores how the plaza could better support comfort, social activity, and neighborhood identity through improved shade,flexible seating, planting, lighting, and public gathering space. Using site analysis, visual research, and AI-assisted concept development, the project compares multiple design directions before combining them into a final hybrid proposal.

The goal is to transform Montefiore Square from a pass-through space into a flexible public destination that supports students, residents, commuters, and community activity throughout the day.

Site / Context

Montefiore Square is a triangular public plaza located at the intersection of Broadway, West 138th Street, and Hamilton Place in Harlem. The site is surrounded by residential buildings, local businesses, and nearby transit, making it a highly visible and accessible public space within the neighborhood.

The physical layout of the plaza is defined by a combination of open paved areas and raised planting zones. A central circular green space with tiered stone seating creates a focal point, while surrounding pathways guide pedestrian movement through the site. The overall layout is open and easy to navigate, but it also leaves large areas exposed and undefined.

The plaza is used by a mix of pedestrians, including commuters passing through, local residents, and nearby students. Movement through the space is generally continuous, with people walking along the paved paths rather than stopping for extended periods. While the site supports circulation effectively, it provides fewer opportunities for people to comfortably stay and use the space.

Although the plaza benefits from its central location and open design, it lacks stronger spatial definition and areas that encourage gathering. The combination of open pavement, limited shade, and fixed seating results in a space that functions more as a passageway than a destination, creating an opportunity for redesign to improve comfort, activity, and overall usability.

The Problem

The existing plaza lacks shade, seating variety, visual identity, and a clear sense of comfort for everyday use. Large areas of open pavement are exposed to direct sunlight, making the space feel harsh and uninviting, especially during warmer parts of the day. This limits how long people are willing to stay and reduces the overall usability of the plaza.

The space is dominated by wide, empty paved areas with very few places designed for people to gather or spend time. As shown in the image, most users pass through the plaza rather than stopping, which suggests that the current layout does not encourage activity or social interaction. The lack of defined zones for seating, gathering, or relaxation makes the plaza feel underused despite its central location.

Seating options are limited and not flexible. There are few comfortable places to sit, and the existing seating does not allow users to choose where to position themselves, such as in shade or near others. This reduces the plaza’s ability to support a variety of users, including students, families, and older residents who may need more comfortable and adaptable seating.

The plaza also lacks a strong visual identity. The materials and layout feel uniform and generic, without distinctive features that reflect the surrounding Harlem community. Without elements such as public art, varied materials, or culturally meaningful design, the space does not stand out as a memorable or engaging public place.

Together, these issues contribute to a space that feels more like a walkway than a destination. Although the plaza is located in a busy and accessible area, it does not currently provide the comfort, flexibility, or activity needed to encourage people to stay, interact, and fully use the space.

Design Options

Option 1: Green Wellness Plaza

This design focuses on creating a calmer, greener, and more comfortable environment for everyday use. It introduces increased tree planting, expanded shade coverage, and more flexible seating to support rest, study, and informal gathering.

The goal is to improve the plaza’s livability by making it more comfortable throughout the day, especially for students, residents, and older users who benefit from shade and quiet seating areas.

Green edges and planted zones help soften the hardscape and reduce the feeling of openness, while varied seating locations allow users to choose between sun, shade, and more private or relaxed settings.

This option prioritizes comfort, shade, and everyday passive use over large-scale events or programmed activity.

Option 2: Community Activity Plaza

This design emphasizes social interaction, flexibility, and public activity. It transforms the plaza into a more active community destination that can support events, informal gatherings, performances, and pop-up uses.

The layout prioritizes open space and adaptable programming, allowing the plaza to shift between everyday use and larger community events depending on time of day or season.

Rather than focusing on quiet retreat, this option strengthens the plaza’s role as a visible and energetic public space within the neighborhood.

Flexible open areas and reduced fixed programming allow the space to accommodate markets, performances, and social gatherings.

This option prioritizes activity, visibility, and community engagement over quiet or passive use.

Final Proposal

The final design brings together the strengths of both design options to create a balanced public space that supports everyday comfort as well as active community use. Instead of choosing between a calm, green environment and a more energetic event space, the proposal organizes Montefiore Square into a layered system where both can coexist without conflict.

The edges of the plaza follow the intent of the Green Wellness Plaza by introducing expanded planting, shade trees, and more comfortable seating conditions. These areas create quieter zones for rest, study, and informal daily use, improving comfort throughout the day and softening the harsh environment. In contrast, the center of the plaza reflects the Community Activity Plaza concept, remaining open and flexible to support gatherings, performances, markets, and other public events.

Together, these two strategies create a single unified space that can adapt to different patterns of use. The perimeter provides calm and shaded areas for everyday life, while the central space supports energy and activity when needed. Montefiore Square becomes a more complete public destination by balancing quiet and active uses, allowing the plaza to function both as a neighborhood retreat and a community gathering space.

Budget & Timeline

The redesign is structured as a phased approach that directly supports the final design concept: a calm, green perimeter surrounding a flexible, active central plaza. Each phase builds the space step-by-step, moving from immediate usability improvements to long-term transformation and programming.

The overall strategy ensures that the shaded, comfortable edges and the active central space are not built separately, but developed together over time as a unified system.

PHASE 1: QUICK IMPROVEMENTS (0–3 months)

Goal: Immediate improvements to comfort and usability across both the plaza edges and central area.

Moveable seating (chairs + tables): $1,500–$4,000

Temporary shade structures (priority along plaza edges): $5,000–$15,000

Wayfinding + signage (to clarify zones and circulation): $2,000–$5,000

Phase 1 Total: $8,500–$24,000

Timeline: 1–3 months

Focus: Improve immediate comfort, encourage people to stay longer, and begin defining calmer edge areas and a usable central gathering space.

PHASE 2: PERMANENT SITE UPGRADES (4–9 months)

Goal: Establish the physical structure of the final design: green, shaded edges and a flexible central plaza.

Tree planting + soil + landscape beds (creates shaded perimeter zones): $20,000–$45,000

Lighting upgrades (improves safety in both edges and center): $10,000–$25,000

Public art / mural installation (builds identity and place-making): $20,000–$60,000

Phase 2 Total: $50,000–$130,000

Timeline: 4–9 months

Focus: Define the plaza’s identity by strengthening shaded green edges, improving nighttime safety, and reinforcing the central area as a flexible gathering space for events and community use.

PHASE 3: PROGRAMMING + MAINTENANCE (ongoing)

Goal: Activate and sustain the plaza as a long-term community destination.

Community programming (markets, events in central plaza): $8,000–$20,000

Maintenance planning (upkeep of green edges + seating zones): $5,000–$15,000

Evaluation + adjustments (improving use over time): $3,000–$10,000

Phase 3 Total: $16,000–$45,000

Timeline: 6–12 months (initial setup), then ongoing

Focus: Ensure the central space stays active through programming while the shaded edges remain comfortable, clean, and usable for everyday life.

Success Metrics

Success is evaluated across the same three phases as the implementation plan. Each phase includes measurable indicators that assess whether the redesign improves comfort, activity, and long-term community use of Montefiore Square.

PHASE 1: QUICK IMPROVEMENTS (0–3 months)

Success in Phase 1 is measured by immediate behavioral changes in how people use the plaza. Key indicators include: increased average dwell time (target: +20–30%), higher seating occupancy during daytime hours, and visible use of temporary shade structures and movable seating. A successful Phase 1 means the plaza begins functioning as a place to stay, not just pass through.

PHASE 2: PERMANENT SITE UPGRADES (4–9 months)

Success in Phase 2 is measured by physical transformation and spatial identity. Indicators include: increased use of shaded perimeter zones, higher occupancy of the central flexible space during programmed and non-programmed hours, improved nighttime visibility and perceived safety due to lighting upgrades, and stronger public recognition of the plaza through landscape and art installations. A successful Phase 2 means the space is clearly readable as a designed public destination.

PHASE 3: PROGRAMMING + LONG-TERM MANAGEMENT

Goal: Establish continuous activation, maintenance, and long-term stewardship of Montefiore Square as a public space.

This phase focuses on the long-term operation of the plaza after physical construction is complete. Rather than being a fixed project stage, Phase 3 represents the ongoing management of the space to ensure it remains active, safe, and well-maintained over time.

Community programming includes recurring events such as seasonal markets, small performances, student gatherings, and neighborhood activities in the central plaza. These programs are intended to maintain consistent public presence and reinforce the plaza as a local destination rather than a pass-through space.

Maintenance operations include routine cleaning, landscaping upkeep, seating repairs, and seasonal planting replacement to preserve the quality of both the shaded perimeter zones and the central flexible space. Lighting systems and safety features are also regularly inspected to ensure continued usability during evening hours.

Ongoing evaluation is conducted to assess how the space is being used over time. This includes monitoring foot traffic patterns, dwell time, seating occupancy, and the effectiveness of programmed events. Adjustments to programming or physical layout may be implemented based on observed usage trends.

Phase 3 Total: $16,000–$45,000 (annual programming and maintenance range)

Timeline: Begins 6–12 months after initial implementation and continues as a permanent operational phase of the plaza.

Focus: Long-term stewardship, sustained public engagement, and adaptive management to ensure Montefiore Square remains a functional and active community space.

Overall success is achieved when Montefiore Square functions as a dual-purpose space: a calm, shaded edge environment for everyday use and an active central hub for community gathering and events.

Reflection / Conclusion

AI was used throughout the design process to quickly generate and test different ideas for Montefiore Square. It helped explore variations in seating, planting, shade, and spatial organization without needing to manually model each version. This made it easier to compare multiple design directions and understand how different choices affect the overall feel and function of the plaza.

However, the AI-generated results were not always realistic or site-specific. Many of the designs felt generic and did not fully respond to the context of Harlem or the actual conditions of Montefiore Square. Some proposals also ignored practical issues such as circulation, accessibility, and how people naturally move and behave in public spaces. Because of this, the outputs had to be carefully evaluated rather than directly used.

The final design was shaped through a combination of AI exploration and personal analysis of the site. The key decision was to combine both proposed directions into one hybrid strategy that balances everyday comfort with flexible community use. Overall, AI supported the early design process, but the final outcome was determined through critical thinking, site understanding, and design judgment.
