Harlem Plaza Redesign

Reimagining Montefiore Square as a safer, greener, and more welcoming public space for residents, visitors, and neighborhood activity.

Explore the Project

About the Project

This project reimagines Montefiore Square as a safer, greener, and more welcoming public space for everyday community life. Located in Harlem at the intersection of Broadway, West 138th Street, and Hamilton Place, the plaza is highly visible and heavily used — yet most people move through it rather than stay in it.

The redesign explores how the plaza could better support comfort, social activity, and neighborhood identity through improved shade,flexible seating, planting, lighting, and public gathering space. Using site analysis, visual research, and AI-assisted concept development, the project compares multiple design directions before combining them into a final hybrid proposal.

The goal is to transform Montefiore Square from a pass-through space into a flexible public destination that supports students, residents, commuters, and community activity throughout the day.

The Problem

The existing plaza lacks shade, seating variety, visual identity, and a clear sense of comfort for everyday use. Large areas of open pavement are exposed to direct sunlight, making the space feel harsh and uninviting, especially during warmer parts of the day. This limits how long people are willing to stay and reduces the overall usability of the plaza.

The space is dominated by wide, empty paved areas with very few places designed for people to gather or spend time. As shown in the image, most users pass through the plaza rather than stopping, which suggests that the current layout does not encourage activity or social interaction. The lack of defined zones for seating, gathering, or relaxation makes the plaza feel underused despite its central location.

Seating options are limited and not flexible. There are few comfortable places to sit, and the existing seating does not allow users to choose where to position themselves, such as in shade or near others. This reduces the plaza’s ability to support a variety of users, including students, families, and older residents who may need more comfortable and adaptable seating.

The plaza also lacks a strong visual identity. The materials and layout feel uniform and generic, without distinctive features that reflect the surrounding Harlem community. Without elements such as public art, varied materials, or culturally meaningful design, the space does not stand out as a memorable or engaging public place.

Together, these issues contribute to a space that feels more like a walkway than a destination. Although the plaza is located in a busy and accessible area, it does not currently provide the comfort, flexibility, or activity needed to encourage people to stay, interact, and fully use the space.

Site / Context

Montefiore Square is a triangular public plaza located at the intersection of Broadway, West 138th Street, and Hamilton Place in Harlem. The site is surrounded by residential buildings, local businesses, and nearby transit, making it a highly visible and accessible public space within the neighborhood.

The physical layout of the plaza is defined by a combination of open paved areas and raised planting zones. A central circular green space with tiered stone seating creates a focal point, while surrounding pathways guide pedestrian movement through the site. The overall layout is open and easy to navigate, but it also leaves large areas exposed and undefined.

The plaza is used by a mix of pedestrians, including commuters passing through, local residents, and nearby students. Movement through the space is generally continuous, with people walking along the paved paths rather than stopping for extended periods. While the site supports circulation effectively, it provides fewer opportunities for people to comfortably stay and use the space.

Although the plaza benefits from its central location and open design, it lacks stronger spatial definition and areas that encourage gathering. The combination of open pavement, limited shade, and fixed seating results in a space that functions more as a passageway than a destination, creating an opportunity for redesign to improve comfort, activity, and overall usability.

Design Options

Option 1: Green Wellness Plaza

This concept introduces more planting, shade trees, flexible seating, and a calmer atmosphere focused on rest, wellness, and everyday use. The goal of this option is to make Montefiore Square feel more comfortable for people who want to sit, study, relax, or take a break from the surrounding streets. By adding greener edges and more inviting seating, the plaza becomes more usable during the day and more supportive of quiet, everyday public life.

This option would serve students, residents, seniors, and families who benefit most from comfort, shade, and a quieter environment. Its main strength is that it improves the plaza’s daily livability, but its limitation is that it does less to create a strong event space or active social destination for larger community gathering.

Option 2: Community Activity Plaza

This concept emphasizes gathering, identity, and flexible space for events and social interaction. Rather than focusing primarily on quiet seating and greenery, this option is designed to make the plaza feel more active and public-facing, with open space that can support vendors, performances, informal social use, and other community activities. The design aims to give the plaza a stronger neighborhood presence and make it feel like a visible destination rather than only a pass-through space.

This option would serve community groups, families, young people, and visitors who benefit from a more social and adaptable public environment. Its main strength is that it creates energy and activity, but its limitation is that it offers less everyday calm and comfort than the wellness-focused option, especially for users who simply want shade, quiet, and flexible seating.

Final Proposal

The final proposal combines the strongest qualities of both design directions by creating a plaza that supports both everyday comfort and active community use. Instead of choosing between a calm green space and a social gathering space, the design organizes the plaza so that different areas serve different functions at the same time.

As visitors enter the plaza from the subway side, the space immediately feels more open and inviting, with clearer pathways guiding movement while still encouraging people to stay. The edges of the plaza are redesigned with expanded planting beds, including trees, shrubs, and flowering plants that create a greener and more comfortable environment. These planted areas help reduce noise from the surrounding streets and provide shade, making the space more usable during the day.

Seating is arranged throughout the plaza using moveable café chairs and tables, allowing people to choose where and how they sit. Some seating is placed near planting areas for a quieter experience, while other areas are more open and social. This flexibility allows students, families, and residents to use the space in different ways, whether they are studying, relaxing, or meeting with others.

At the center of the plaza, a flexible open space is maintained to support community activity. This area can be used for everyday gathering, but it also allows for events such as markets, performances, or local programming. By keeping this space open, the plaza can adapt to different needs throughout the day and week without feeling overcrowded or empty.

The design also strengthens the identity of the plaza through visual elements such as warmer paving materials, organized seating layouts, and the potential for public art. These features help the space feel more connected to the surrounding community rather than like a generic urban plaza.

In the evening, improved lighting helps extend the usability of the space and makes it feel safer and more welcoming. Overall, the final proposal creates a balanced environment that supports both quiet everyday use and active social life, making Montefiore Square a more functional and meaningful public space.

Community Context

Montefiore Square serves a diverse group of users including local residents, City College students, commuters, and families. Because the site sits near transit and in a dense neighborhood setting, it functions as both a pass-through space and a local public gathering area. The design of the plaza therefore needs to respond to different patterns of use throughout the day rather than serving only one type of visitor.

Students from nearby City College are likely to use the plaza between classes for sitting, eating, and meeting friends. Commuters move through the site more quickly, especially near peak travel times, and benefit from clear circulation and a welcoming entry sequence. Residents and families use the space more casually, whether as a shortcut, a place to pause, or a place to gather with others in the neighborhood.

A successful redesign must support all of these users at once. That is why the final proposal combines quieter seating and planting areas with open community space, rather than treating the plaza as only a landscaped park or only an event space. The goal is to create a public space that feels useful, comfortable, and meaningful to the people who pass through it every day.

Budget & Timeline

This proposal can be imagined as a phased redesign rather than a single all-at-once construction project. Smaller, faster improvements such as moveable seating, shade elements, and wayfinding could be introduced first to quickly improve comfort and visibility. Larger interventions such as planting upgrades, lighting, and public art could then be added over time as funding and partnerships develop.

A conceptual budget for the project includes approximately $18,000 to $28,000 for moveable seating, $30,000 to $55,000 for shade structures, $35,000 to $60,000 for planting enhancements, $10,000 to $20,000 for lighting improvements, and $40,000 to $75,000 for a mural or other public art feature. Together, these interventions suggest a realistic conceptual cost range for a phased public-space improvement project.

In terms of timeline, Phase 1 would focus on quick changes such as seating, signage, and temporary shade. Phase 2 would include more permanent landscape and lighting improvements, as well as visual identity features such as public art. Phase 3 would focus on long-term programming, maintenance, and evaluation to make sure the plaza continues to serve the surrounding community effectively over time.

Success Metrics

The success of the redesign would be measured by whether more people choose to stay in the plaza rather than simply pass through it. One important indicator would be increased dwell time, meaning that users spend longer periods sitting, relaxing, studying, or gathering in the space after the redesign is introduced.

Additional success metrics would include more daily seating use, greater activity in the plaza during midday and evening hours, and more frequent informal or organized community use. If the redesigned space is working well, it should feel more active, more comfortable, and more visually distinctive than it does in its current condition.

Other signs of success would include stronger shade coverage, better use of flexible gathering space, and a clearer sense of neighborhood identity through planting, materials, and public art. Together, these metrics would help show whether the redesign has improved both the function and the experience of Montefiore Square.

Reflection / Conclusion

AI played an important role in the development of this project by helping me quickly explore and visualize different design possibilities for Montefiore Square. I used AI image generation tools to create before-and-after renderings of the plaza, as well as ChatGPT to help organize ideas and improve written sections. Through multiple iterations, I tested different prompts to compare how changes in greenery, seating, and activity could transform the space.

One of the main strengths of AI in this project was speed and visualization. It allowed me to generate several design options and compare them side by side without needing to fully draw or model each version manually. This made it easier to experiment with different levels of planting, shade, and seating arrangements, and to better understand how those changes would affect the overall atmosphere of the plaza.

However, AI also had limitations. Many of the generated images looked visually appealing but felt generic and not specific to Harlem or Montefiore Square. Some designs were unrealistic in terms of how people would actually use the space, or did not fully consider movement, accessibility, or real-world conditions. Because of this, I had to carefully evaluate and reject some of the AI-generated ideas rather than using them directly.

The most important decisions in this project required my own judgment. I had to decide how to balance quiet, everyday use with more active community gathering, and how the design should respond to the needs of students, residents, and visitors. The decision to combine both design options into a hybrid proposal was based on understanding that the plaza needs to support multiple uses at different times of day.

Overall, AI was a useful tool for generating ideas and speeding up the design process, but it was not a replacement for critical thinking. The final proposal reflects a combination of AI-assisted exploration and my own decisions about what would work best for the site and the community.
